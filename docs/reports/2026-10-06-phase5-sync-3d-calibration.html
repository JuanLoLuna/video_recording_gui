Video Recorder Phase 5

Two cameras measured in 3D: how far apart in time their frames really are, a heartbeat LED that puts both on the laptop's clock, and calibration built into the recorder, validated on the rig at 1–3 mm.

Summary

Phase 4 made the recorder run two cameras reliably. This phase asked what it takes to measure hands in 3D with them. There are two halves to that. The cameras have to be aligned in time, and their positions and lenses have to be known.

The second half is done and validated on the rig. Each camera is calibrated in the recorder; each session is set up from a board in about a minute; a live check notices if a camera is bumped; and an independent recording measured the result at 0.7–1.4 mm shape error, with the worst single corner off by 2.8 mm. The first half is measured but not solved. The cameras free-run, so their frames are up to half a frame apart. The heartbeat LED puts every frame on one clock, and simultaneous exposure needs an external trigger pulse, which is the next piece of work.

The gap covers the whole possible range, from zero to half a frame, and passes through every value every one to two minutes. It is not clock drift: the two camera clocks agree to better than 1 ppm. It comes from the Firefly's frame-rate setting, which cannot reach exactly 30.000 or 60.000 fps and runs about 0.035 % slow. A hand moving at 1 m/s travels up to 17 mm between the two views at 30 fps, so triangulating fast movement needs either simultaneous exposures or interpolated keypoints.

Two smaller results came from the same run. The timestamp_us column in the metadata holds nanoseconds; the column keeps its name because downstream code reads it by name. And the laptop arrival time (system_time) jitters by 2–4 ms, with bursts up to 300 ms at the start of a recording, so it is too noisy to pair frames; the camera timestamps mapped to the laptop clock are accurate to 40–120 µs.

Both cameras can be triggered from a line, so an external pulse (one source driving both) or a cable from the Firefly's exposure output to the Blackfly's Line3 would both work. The Blackfly is the better camera to trigger, because it can start a new exposure during readout and can delay its exposure to line up with the Firefly's.

Two independent methods, the clock latch and the LED, give the same camera clock rates within the LED fit's uncertainty, and the same inter-camera gap. The 60 fps recording also ran the real recorder at about 100 MB/s of uncompressed video from both cameras for 12 minutes, with every one of its 25 video files decoding completely and no missing frames. Calibration in the recorder

A Calibrate… button opens a separate window with two tasks. Calibrate a camera is done once per camera, and again if anyone touches the lens. Set up for this session is done each time the cameras are moved, and takes about a minute. Both passed on the rig: both cameras' lens calibrations came out under the 0.5 px limit, and the session setup passed verification once its limits matched the rig's noise (finding 4).

The boards had to fit this room. The cameras sit on opposite sides of the desk, 2.83 m apart and about 125° apart around the work area, so one flat board is seen steeply by at least one of them, and the Firefly is low-resolution.

What broke, and how it’s handled

01The two cameras' frames slide through every possible offset
Symptom
The gap between the cameras' frames was spread evenly from 0 to half a frame, cycling every 1–1.6 minutes.
Cause
→ The Firefly's frame-rate setting cannot hit exactly 30 or 60 fps; the camera clocks themselves agree to better than 1 ppm.
Found by
The sync probe's per-frame camera timestamps, confirmed independently through the heartbeat LED.
Result
Handled for alignment, open for simultaneity: the LED puts every frame on the laptop's clock; simultaneous exposures need the external trigger pulse (open items).
02The setup's own 3D check could not catch a wrong lens
Symptom
In simulation, a 20 % focal-length error still passed the setup's 3D check with under 1 mm of error.
Cause
→ Both camera positions are solved from the same board that is then measured, so they stay consistent with each other even when wrong.
Found by
Tests that render boards through deliberately wrong lenses.
Result
Fixed by design: a separate verification measures the board after it moves at least 15 cm toward the cameras, using the saved positions; a 10 % focal error then shows as about 2 % size error.
03The boards were too small or too steep for one of the cameras
Symptom
The Firefly could not read board A anywhere the Blackfly could also see it, and could read a reference board only where the subject sits.
Cause
→ The cameras face each other across the desk, so a shared board is steep to at least one; at the Firefly's 720×540, board A's markers were about 13 px, about 2 px per marker cell. One snapshot was also 22 % saturated.
Found by
The diagnosis snapshots, read here instead of guessed from photos.
Result
Fixed: board A no longer has to lie flat; A-big for the setup (1.55× bigger marker cells); G1/G2 reference boards readable from about 3× farther; exposure lowered.
04Verification failed by small margins, again and again
Symptom
The setup verification kept failing just over its limits.
Cause
→ With about 6 shared corners at about 0.4 px of noise, a correct calibration failed the original 1 % / 2 mm limits in 26–75 % of simulated attempts.
Found by
Simulating the rig's geometry and noise with a known-correct calibration.
Result
Fixed: limits of 2 % size and 3 mm shape (4 mm over a 20 cm hand span), 3 s of frames averaged instead of 1.2 s, and a log of every attempt. Passed on the rig.
05The code audit's two serious findings
Symptom
Closing the Calibration window with Esc left its detection thread running through later recordings. A board seen as a single row of corners was captured and then made Compute fail with no way out.
Cause
→ Esc hides a Qt dialog without its close event, where the cleanup was; and a single row has exactly the minimum corner count on the A4 boards.
Found by
An independent code audit, before any rig session.
Result
Fixed, along with the 11 smaller findings, each with a test.

08

Where this leaves things

The geometry side of 3D is ready: two cameras on opposite sides of the desk measure a still object to within a few millimetres, the recorder keeps every session's calibration with the video, and it notices when a camera moves. What stands between this and 3D hand tracking is time. Every frame can already be placed on one clock, but until the cameras expose together, fast movements will be blurred across the two views by up to a frame. The external trigger pulse is the next piece of work.
